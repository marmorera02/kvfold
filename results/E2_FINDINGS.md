## Two independent, reproducible root causes

### 1. z_L2 does not track the contradiction — and is consistently inverted for it

N2's z_L2 is **negative in all three seeds** (-1.63, -1.58, -0.67) — the section containing the contradiction reads as *less* surprising than average at the coarse level, the opposite of the theorized spike. This is not z-scoring noise: the raw (non-standardized) mean_surprisal for that section is also the lowest or near-lowest of the 10 sections in every seed I inspected directly (seed 0: section 9 raw mean 1.475 bits/token vs. a 10-section spread of 1.48–3.84).

z_L0 also doesn't match theory for N2: it's supposed to be invisible (< 1) at the token level, but it's consistently *moderately* elevated (~1.35–1.47) across all three seeds — not as high as N1/N3 (~2.3–2.55), but reliably above the boilerplate baseline (N4: -1.9 to 0.6). So N2 sits in a stable, real, but *intermediate* zone at L0 — not literally invisible, just quieter than the other two needle types (this matches what E1 already found).

**Likely cause:** L1/L2/L3 summaries are generated by the same 3B model that later scores them (both under greedy, seed-pinned decoding). N2's underlying contradiction is phrased in extremely common legal boilerplate register ("notwithstanding any other provision... shall be unlimited... not subject to the limitations set forth herein") — exactly the kind of fluent, high-frequency legal phrasing this model reproduces easily, whether it's writing it (summarization) or reading it back (scoring).

One clarification from checking the *document-wide* (not just needle) mean surprisal per level: L2 as a category is **not** uniformly low-perplexity — its overall mean (~2.6–2.7 bits/token across seeds) is actually higher than both L0 (~1.8) and L1 (~1.1–1.2). So the effect isn't "generated text is always predictable to its own author" in general. It's narrower and, I think, more interesting: *within* the L2 distribution specifically, the section containing N2 is a consistent outlier toward the *low* end (below the L2 median in all three seeds), because that section's summary — dominated by a common, high-precedent legal-exception template — is easier for the model to both produce and re-read than the other nine sections, which have to compress more heterogeneous, less-templated content into their 2–3 sentences. The anomaly is relative-within-L2, not absolute-across-levels. N3 (novel/off-topic) shows the same relative pattern (z_L2 never clearly elevated: 0.64, -0.04, -0.15), suggesting this "the contradiction's surface form is unremarkable to summarize and unremarkable to re-score" effect isn't unique to logical contradictions — genuinely novel content doesn't reliably propagate an elevated signal to L2 either. N1's z_L2 is the one place without a stable direction (1.69 / 0.13 / -0.10) — noisy rather than biased, for reasons I haven't isolated.

### 2. The NLI verifier has a ~65% false-positive "contradiction" rate on this corpus

Across all three seeds, 65–67% of *all* L1 units (nearly all ordinary boilerplate) score ≥0.5 max-contradiction against their top-5 nearest neighbors, and roughly half score ≥0.9:

| Seed | mean | median | frac ≥0.5 | frac ≥0.9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.628 | 0.864 | 0.65 | 0.48 |
| 1 | 0.673 | 0.967 | 0.67 | 0.59 |
| 2 | 0.647 | 0.941 | 0.65 | 0.53 |

The median is *above* 0.5 in every seed — meaning the NLI model calls the *majority* of ordinary clauses "more likely contradictory than not." I confirmed the mechanism by hand: e.g. seed 0, clause 89 ("Prices for industrial control modules... due 60 days...") vs. its top retrieved premise, clause 29 ("Prices for calibrated pressure sensors... due 30 days...") — two *independent* payment clauses about *different products*, scored 0.998 contradiction. This is a well-documented brittleness of NLI models trained on datasets like MNLI/FEVER/ANLI: structurally-similar sentences that differ in a number or named entity are frequently classified as contradicting, even when they're unrelated statements about different things. Our corpus reuses 16 templates across ~94 boilerplate clauses with only slot values (numbers, products, currencies) varying — precisely the pattern that triggers this heuristic. NLI *does* correctly flag the true contradiction (0.99 every time) — but it flags almost everything else just as confidently, so it has no discriminating power as implemented. A higher threshold doesn't fix it either: the false positives are just as extreme (many at 0.998+) as the true positive (0.99).

This is why "self-generated text is universally low-perplexity" isn't the right frame — the real effect is specific to where a given section falls *within* the L2 distribution.

## Why this is a structural failure, not an adjacency bug

Spec's decision tree offers "fix windows, rerun once" for the case where the cap-frame and exception don't share an L2 scoring window. That's not the issue here: my L0→L2 scoring conditions each section on *all preceding sections* in one continuous context, so the section containing N2 (section 9) does have the cap-frame (section 1) in its input context when scored — the adjacency exists in the sense the spec means, and it still doesn't produce a spike. The failure is in the scoring mechanism itself (self-generated text reads as fluent to its own author regardless of context) and in the verifier (NLI's numeric-mismatch brittleness on templated text), not in how far apart the clauses are.

## What would need to change to retest this

1. **Break the self-scoring loop**: generate summaries with a different model (or the same model at a different, less "self-referential" operating point) than the one used to score them, so surprisal isn't measuring "would I have written this" but genuine cross-model surprise.
2. **Fix NLI's false-positive problem**: restrict candidate premises to clauses that share a real referent (e.g., same defined term, same clause cross-reference) rather than pure embedding nearest-neighbors, which currently just retrieves same-template near-duplicates; or accept that NLI needs a much more targeted comparison set than "top-5 by cosine" on a templated corpus.
3. **Reduce template repetition in the corpus** (or accept it as a deliberate limitation): the ~94 heavily-slotted boilerplate clauses are exactly what triggers NLI's numeric-mismatch heuristic; a corpus with more lexically-varied (not just slot-varied) boilerplate would be a fairer test of the NLI stage specifically.

None of these are quick fixes, so per spec ("E2 FAILS structurally → stop building; the scale-space claim needs reformulation"), I stopped here rather than proceeding to E3/E4 on an unvalidated foundation.
