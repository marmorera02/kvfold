The lighthouse keeper had kept the same logbook for eleven winters, and every entry began the same way: wind direction, barometric pressure, the state of the tide, and then whatever else seemed worth setting down before the light went dark again at dawn. He believed that most of what mattered about the sea could not be measured directly, only inferred from the small discrepancies between what the instruments predicted and what actually happened overnight. A gull blown two miles off its usual course. A swell arriving four minutes early. A silence in the water where the current should have been audible against the rocks. None of these were emergencies. Most of them meant nothing at all. But he wrote them down anyway, because the ones that meant something never announced themselves in advance, and the only way to catch them later was to have a record of everything that came before.

His predecessor had told him, on the last night of the handover, that the job was not really about the light. The light ran itself; the mechanism was simple and had not failed in forty years. The job, she said, was noticing. Noticing when the fog came in from the wrong quarter. Noticing when a fishing boat that always passed at eleven was forty minutes late, and knowing whether forty minutes was ordinary drift or the beginning of a story that would end with a search party. She had kept a second notebook, separate from the official log, where she wrote down only the things that felt wrong without being able to say why. Most nights it stayed empty. On the nights it did not stay empty, she said, you learned to trust it completely, because by the time you could explain the feeling in words the moment that mattered had usually already passed.

He kept both notebooks after that. The official log filled up steadily, one page a night, wind and pressure and tide, clause after clause of a contract with the ocean that never quite got broken and never quite got renewed either. The second notebook filled up much more slowly, sometimes a single line across two weeks, sometimes three entries in one night when the weather turned strange all at once. He noticed that the entries in the second notebook rarely matched the drama in the first. A night with gale-force winds and driving rain could pass without a single line in the private book, because everything about it, however unpleasant, was exactly what the season predicted. Meanwhile an unremarkable Tuesday with calm seas and a light southerly breeze might earn three lines, because a container ship that should have passed the point by midnight was still visible on the horizon at two, moving at a quarter of its normal speed, for no reason the log could account for.

By his third winter he had stopped trying to explain the second notebook to visitors. It was not a record of danger. It was a record of surprise, and surprise turned out to be a much narrower and much stranger category than danger was. Storms were rarely surprising; the barometer told you they were coming days in advance, and everyone on the coast prepared accordingly. What was surprising was the ordinary thing behaving in a way that did not fit the pattern it had established for itself over years of observation, quietly, without any accompanying violence at all. He came to think that this was probably true of most systems worth watching closely, not just the sea, and that the people who were good at his job were not the ones who reacted best to storms but the ones who noticed the quiet Tuesday that did not add up, and who trusted that feeling long enough to write it down before it passed.
